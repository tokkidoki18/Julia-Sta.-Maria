When I take a photograph, I am usually trying to hold on to something, whether that may be a moment, a feeling, or a detail that I might otherwise forget. Photography gives me a way to pay attention to my surroundings and decide what matters to me. Reading Susan Sontag's "In Plato's Cave," however, made me question whether taking a picture always helps me appreciate an experience. Sometimes, the desire to perfectly capture a moment can become more important than actually being present in it.

One of the ideas that stood out to me is Sontag's argument that photographs are interpretations of the world. It is easy to think of a photograph as proof because it shows something that existed in front of a camera. However, the photographer still chooses the angle, timing, framing, and which image to keep. These choices affect the viewers' reactions and understanding of the photograph. The same place or person can appear completely different depending on how they are photographed. Even without editing the image, the photographer already shapes its meaning by deciding what belongs inside the frame.

I think this makes social media extremely relevant. Different social platforms online often portray someone's life. A photograph can capture a happy moment without showing the stress or uncertainty surrounding it. I do not think that makes the positive emotions false, but it does make the picture incomplete and, in a way, inauthentic. As someone who both views and consumes this form of online content, I want to be more aware of that gap and bias.

To interpret the current state of the world, I would be interested in exploring the relationship between what people show and what they experience privately, with absolute consideration for online discretion and safety, of course. Photography could capture everyday spaces, expressions, and small details, while illustration or text could introduce feelings that are not immediately visible. For example, models and public figures often post more photographs than text-focused content. Meanwhile, companies often mix images with creative illustrations and witty captions to market their products to consumers.

Throughout the semester, I would like my projects to connect through attention to everyday life and the differences between appearance and experience. Even when the tools or formats change, I could return to questions about what an image reveals, what it leaves out, and how an audience might interpret it. Each project could help me become more deliberate about those directions.

Photography and image making offer me a way to communicate a perspective, a feeling, or an idea. However, these also require me to recognize their limits. I want to create work that encourages someone to pause, notice the details, or question an initial impression. Sontag's chapter encourages me to think beyond whether an image simply looks appealing to the viewer or if it delivers the feeling or idea I am trying to show. That is an approach I hope to develop as I learn new ways of making images and connecting them to the world around me.

After reading Your Brain on ChatGPT and watching Brain Experts WARNING: Watch This Before Using ChatGPT Again!, I started thinking more about how artificial intelligence has become part of everyday life, especially for students. I personally use ChatGPT pretty frequently for school, whether I am studying, trying to understand a difficult concept, organizing information, or getting feedback on something I have written. As a psychology major, I found the topic really interesting because it connects to concepts I study, such as memory, learning, attention, and cognitive processing. The MIT study found differences in brain activity between people who completed an essay using an LLM, a search engine, or only their own knowledge. It also found that participants who relied on an LLM had more difficulty recalling what they had written. Reading about these results made me think about how relying too heavily on AI could affect how deeply we process and remember information.

However, I still believe that AI is a great tool to have. I do not think the technology itself is necessarily the problem. Instead, I think the bigger issue is how people choose to use it. AI should be a tool that supports our abilities rather than a replacement for them. Nowadays, it can be tempting to immediately turn to AI to generate an answer, complete an assignment, or solve a problem without first trying to understand it ourselves. While this may save time, it can also take away the mental effort involved in actually learning something. This relates to the idea of cognitive debt discussed in the materials. If we constantly allow AI to do the difficult thinking for us, we may become dependent on it rather than developing our own understanding.

I have noticed this difference in the way I use ChatGPT myself. When I simply ask for an answer and read it, I might understand it in the moment, but I am less likely to remember it later. On the other hand, when I first try to understand something myself and then use ChatGPT to clarify what I got wrong, explain a confusing concept differently, or quiz me, I am much more engaged. This is especially useful for me as a psychology major because there are many theories and concepts that require more than memorizing definitions. I need to understand how they work and be able to apply them independently. In that situation, AI can actually help my learning rather than replace it.

The article and video did not make me view AI negatively. Instead, they made me more aware of the responsibility that comes with using it. I think AI is an extremely useful resource, and I will continue using it for school and in my everyday life. However, I also think there needs to be a balance. Having access to a tool that can immediately provide information does not mean we should stop challenging ourselves to think, learn, and solve problems independently. As a psychology student, these materials made me more conscious of my own cognitive habits and how technology can influence them. My biggest takeaway is that AI is most beneficial when it works alongside our thinking, not when it replaces our ability to critically think altogether.

After exploring the website, I chose to focus on Heela's story, My Stolen Future. The story is part of a series that highlights the struggles women face during pregnancy and childbirth in Sierra Leone. What caught my attention was how the creators used illustrations alongside photographs, text, and video to tell these women's stories. I think this combination was effective because it allowed readers to understand not only the situations these women experienced but also the emotions surrounding them. Heela's story especially stood out to me because of its focus on a young girl's future and how circumstances beyond her control can change the direction of her life.

One thing I noticed about the illustrations was how different they felt compared to traditional photographs. Photographs capture real people and environments as they appear, while illustrations allow artists to recreate moments and emphasize certain details. Through the use of colors, facial expressions, and the arrangement of scenes, illustrations can communicate emotions that might be difficult to capture through photography alone. In Heela's story, I think this approach is meaningful because the title itself, My Stolen Future, suggests a loss of opportunities and the life she might have imagined for herself. Illustrations allow readers to visualize these experiences in a way that feels more personal rather than simply reading about them.

Another reason I think illustrations were used is because of the sensitive nature of the story. The series addresses issues involving pregnancy, healthcare, and the challenges women face in Sierra Leone. These are personal experiences, and photographing every moment might not always be appropriate or possible. Illustrations provide an alternative way to represent difficult situations without exposing the people involved to unnecessary attention. They also give the artists more freedom to portray memories, emotions, and events that may not have been captured on camera. I think this is an important advantage because it allows the story to remain emotionally powerful while still respecting the dignity of the individuals involved.

Compared to text alone, illustrations also make the story easier to follow and more engaging. Written descriptions can explain what happened, but visual storytelling helps readers imagine the environment and connect with the people involved. I found that the graphic novel format made the story feel more personal because the illustrations encouraged me to pay attention to the experiences being described rather than just the information being presented. At the same time, photographs serve a different purpose by showing the actual people and conditions behind the story. I think using both forms of media creates a balance between documenting reality and communicating emotions.

I think the use of illustrations in Heela's story was an effective storytelling choice. Rather than replacing photography or text, the illustrations added another perspective that helped communicate the emotional impact of her experiences. This project showed me that illustrations can serve a much bigger purpose than simply making an article visually appealing. They can help tell stories that are difficult to document, encourage empathy, and bring attention to important social issues. For me, the combination of different visual elements made Heela's story more memorable and demonstrated how powerful illustration can be as a form of journalism.
